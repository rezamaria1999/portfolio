Prior authorization evidence assistant

An independent exercise. A system that gathers the evidence a health plan already holds and puts it in front of the clinician reviewing a coverage request, before the decision rather than after the appeal.

The short version

Health plans require prior authorization before certain procedures. A request comes in with clinical records attached, a reviewer checks it against published coverage criteria, and a decision goes out on a regulatory clock.

When one of those requests is denied for insufficient documentation, the documentation is often not missing at all. It's on page nine of a forty-page fax from a practice the member left months ago, or in the plan's own claims data, because the plan already paid for the treatment it's now saying wasn't documented.

That's a search problem, run against a clock, over long unstructured records from several sources. Which is specific enough to suggest both a solution and its limits.

Why this problem

Only about 12% of denials get appealed, and roughly 80% of those are overturned. The other 88% are never looked at again. A plan only hears about its mistakes from the people who push back, so most of its errors are invisible to it by design. And whether a member pushes back depends on their literacy, their energy and whether anyone is helping them file, which means first-decision accuracy is currently rationed by who can fight for it.

What makes it tractable is that nothing new has to be collected. No vendor, no regulatory change, no new pipeline. The discharge summary is already in the packet and the claims are already in the ledger. That turns a question of clinical judgment into one of retrieval over messy text, which is the narrow thing language models are actually good at.

I chose on three things: whether the failure was documented by someone independent, whether the data to fix it already sat inside the organisation, and whether a wrong answer reached a member. Advanced imaging was the only area where all three held.

Who this is for

Two people touch a request before it can become a denial, and what separates them isn't seniority. It's what each is allowed to do.

The nurse reviewer sees every request in the queue. She applies the criteria and she can approve, but she cannot issue a denial on medical necessity, so anything she can't substantiate gets routed onward. The medical director picks up that routed pile against a seven-day clock. He isn't a persona I invented, either. CMS requires him: an adverse medical-necessity determination has to be reviewed by an appropriately qualified clinician before it's issued. So this adds no step and no approver. It changes what's on screen at a review that already happens and legally has to.

Routing by certainty

That split maps onto the architecture. The deterministic layers produce facts, and a nurse can act on a paid claim without making a clinical judgment, so those findings surface at her stage and can end a case in approval before it ever escalates. The model layer produces readings, which carry citations and doubt, and those go to the physician who's equipped to catch a subtle mismatch. A tool that only argues for approval sits naturally with someone who is only permitted to approve.

What happens today

A 72-year-old member, chronic low back pain, lumbar MRI requested. Here's the path it takes, and the path it takes when the evidence is surfaced instead.

Forty-one days that don't happen, and an appeal nobody has to file. Nothing new was submitted in either version.

The evidence was in the building on day 0, in three separate places. Eight paid therapy claims in the plan's own system. A discharge summary on page 9 of the fax recording that zero of three functional goals were met. An anti-inflammatory still active on the medication list.

Nobody made a mistake. The nurse followed policy, the medical director reviewed what was in front of him, and day 7 counts as a success in every operating metric the plan runs. The clock was met. The letter went out on time.

Where the AI goes, and where it doesn't

The governing coverage rule reads: "MRI will be covered only if the patient has not responded to a reasonable trial of conservative management lasting at least four weeks."

One sentence, two conditions, and they're completely different kinds of question.

  • "lasting at least four weeks" belongs to the claims ledger. Did we pay for therapy, and across how many weeks? That's a transaction record. Certain, auditable, costs a database query.
  • "has not responded" has no field anywhere. You can't query for didn't get better. It lives in prose, in a different document every time.

So the layers run cheapest and most certain first, and the model goes last, on the one half nothing else can reach. Most coverage criteria have this shape, a ledger fact welded to a clinical judgment, which is why adding a criterion means adding a document rather than writing code.

What it isn't allowed to do

  • It can't deny. No code path runs from the model to an adverse determination. Not a policy or a threshold, just absent from the architecture.
  • It can't quote something that isn't there. Every finding carries a verbatim quotation, string-matched against its source before display, and failures are dropped before anyone sees them. About ten lines of code.
  • It can't settle a disagreement. When the claims layer and the model contradict each other, both appear and the clinician decides. Quietly siding with the stronger source would be simpler and usually right, and would bury the signal that marks a case as genuinely ambiguous.
  • It can't imply a denial by going quiet. When nothing is found the wording is fixed: "No supporting evidence identified in the submitted record. This is not a coverage recommendation." Silence already carries weight here, and phrasing that read as "nothing found, safe to deny" would make the system a participant in denials through the back door.

How I'd know if it worked

A demo shows a system succeeding once. An evaluation tells you how often it fails and in which direction, which is the part worth knowing before it touches anyone.

On 34 cases the prototype runs 83% precision and 83% recall. The interval matters more than the headline: 95% Wilson on recall, at six cases containing findable support, runs from 44% to 97%. That covers everything from barely better than chance to near perfect, so it isn't really a measurement.

So I built a labelled probe set and a runner that scores model output against it, broken out by failure class rather than pooled into one flattering number. Each probe exercises a specific way the system can go wrong: therapy for the wrong body region, a patient who actually got better, therapy that hasn't happened yet, therapy explicitly declined, a trial too short to count, a page belonging to a different member, a real find buried in illegible handwriting.

Precision goes from 25% to 100% because every false positive on that slice was catchable by a join. A body region, a member ID, a date span. None of them needed the model to be more careful. Recall doesn't move, because it's a different problem.

The failure I left in

One of the three demo cases is one the system gets wrong, and I kept it there deliberately. A member's record holds twenty-one paid therapy claims spanning eight weeks, a complete and entirely authentic therapy record. Every claim carries a diagnosis pointer for a knee replacement. The request is about a lumbar complaint.

The model reports the therapy as evidence of conservative management. The quotation is real, verbatim, on the page it says it's on. The citation guard passes it, correctly.

This isn't a hallucination. The guard checks provenance, whether the text exists where it claims to. What failed is relevance, whether authentic evidence bears on this criterion for this request. I'd built a verifier for one of those and not the other.

It never became a wrong decision, because the architecture can't produce one, and it surfaced to a clinician who can reject knee-versus-back in about two seconds. What it costs is reviewer time and trust. Let precision drop far enough and reviewers stop reading the flags, at which point real recall is zero whatever the offline number says.

What it costs, and what I don't know

Priced as cost avoidance the business case is weak, and I'd rather say so than have a CFO find it. Most wrongly denied members never appeal, so today the plan doesn't pay for that care. Getting more first decisions right means paying for care it currently doesn't, which makes the same population both the cost and the return. The real case rests on decision quality now that it's published, and on a system that architecturally cannot produce an unreviewed denial at a moment when that fact pattern is in discovery across the industry.

What I don't know is larger. I haven't spoken to a single reviewer. All of this is inferred from regulation, public audit data and reasoning about how the workflow probably runs, and I'd expect at least one assumption not to survive contact with the job. The first thing I'd test is the premise itself, that evidence is buried and reviewers can't find it. Two weeks and a query against appeal files the plan already holds settles it, before anyone builds anything.

Evaluating the evidence reader

How you find out whether a model reading clinical records is any good, and how you stop it surfacing evidence that has nothing to do with the criterion being checked.

Code on GitHub ↗ evaluate.py structural_checks.py eval-set.json

What's actually being predicted

The first mistake available here is measuring the wrong thing. This system doesn't opine on whether a request should be approved, so scoring it against the coverage decision would be scoring it on a job it isn't doing.

The unit is narrower: per case, per criterion condition, does findable supporting evidence exist in this record, and where is it? That "and where" matters more than it looks. A label that only says yes-or-no can't tell apart a model that found the right passage from one that found a passage that happens to sit in a case where support exists. So every label carries a document, a page and the span that counts.

And each layer gets scored against the job it actually has. A condition the claims ledger settled isn't subject to model error and doesn't belong in the model's denominator. Pooling them would flatter the model by crediting it with the database's work, and would hide a claims-layer failure inside a healthy-looking aggregate. In practice that's three numbers, not one: claims coverage and correctness, narrative precision and recall, and the conflict rate between them. The third is the one nobody instruments by default, and a rising conflict rate is early warning that something upstream moved, whether that's a coding practice, a new document source, or the model itself.

Building the gold set

To know whether "support is on page nine" is true, you need cases where a physician independently determined what the right answer was. That's a gold set, and it has to be built rather than found.

The method is the one HHS OIG used to produce the 13% figure this whole project leans on. Draw a random sample of denied requests in the service line. Have two physician reviewers independently decide whether criteria were met and where the supporting evidence sits, if anywhere. Send disagreements to a third. Track how often the first two agreed.

At roughly twenty minutes per case per reviewer, 500 cases runs to about 333 clinician-hours. That's the real cost of this project and it belongs in the plan as a staffing line, not an assumption. Two physicians at 20% time is 21 weeks. Four at 25% is eight.

Why it can't be sampled from appeals

Sampling appealed cases would be faster, cheaper, and wrong in a predictable direction. Only about 11.5% of denials get appealed, and appealing correlates with health literacy, English proficiency, having an advocate, and having a practice with dedicated prior-auth staff.

A gold set drawn from appeals tells you how the system performs for people who fight. It tells you nothing about the 88% who don't, who are the entire reason this product exists.

This is the same trap that killed my first design. That version ranked denials by predicted overturn-on-appeal, using appeal outcomes as the label, which would have taught the model to predict who pushes back rather than which decisions were wrong. Random sampling costs more. It's the point.

The ceiling nobody reports

Tracking inter-rater agreement isn't housekeeping. It's the ceiling. If two physicians only agree 78% of the time on whether a criterion is met, then a model scoring 83% against a single adjudicator's labels isn't beating anything, it's agreeing with one doctor. Performance should be reported against that human ceiling rather than against 100%, and the data to do it comes free with the adjudication process.

The adversarial slice

A random sample tells you about the common cases. It won't give you enough of the rare ones that destroy reviewer trust, because by definition they're rare. So those get constructed separately, one probe per named failure class, and the two sets are never pooled: mixing a deliberately hard slice into a random sample wrecks any prevalence estimate you wanted from it.

Eight probes, each a specific way to be wrong:

  • Wrong body region. An eight-week therapy course, fully documented, treating a shoulder. The request is lumbar.
  • Patient responded. Lumbar, recent, seven weeks. All four functional goals met and pain down from 7/10 to 2/10. The duration half of the criterion passes and the non-response half fails.
  • Not completed. "The patient will begin a six-week course... we anticipate symptoms will not respond." Every keyword, all of it in the future.
  • Negated. "The patient declined physical therapy. No course of conservative management was undertaken." Quoting the preceding sentence alone would pass verification and invert the meaning.
  • Too short. Genuine lumbar non-response across thirteen days, against a four-week bar.
  • Wrong patient. A misfiled page in a faxed bundle. Different name, different date of birth, different member ID, perfectly authentic lumbar language.
  • Illegible. Real evidence in handwritten shorthand from a bad scan.
  • Clean positive. The control, to prove the harness can still detect a true find.

The harness reports detection with Wilson intervals, localisation, citation fidelity, relevance, abstention quality and concern calibration, broken out by slice. It also prints its own limits: constructed slice, don't pool, n is tiny, human ceiling unmeasured. A number that doesn't carry its caveat gets quoted without it eventually.

Stopping irrelevant evidence

Here's the distinction the whole section turns on. The citation guard proves provenance: this text exists, verbatim, on the page it claims. It says nothing about relevance: whether that authentic text bears on this criterion for this request. Knee therapy is real therapy. It just isn't conservative management for a lumbar complaint.

Five things address that, cheapest first. Two are built.

  1. Built and measured
    Deterministic structural checks

    Body region, member identity and trial duration, all resolved by lookup before any model output reaches a reviewer. The request carries a lumbar diagnosis code, the therapy claims carry a knee one, and that comparison is a join rather than a judgment. On the adversarial slice this moves precision from 25% to 100%, catching all three false positives.

    One subtlety that matters: the duration check fires only when the ledger affirmatively shows a too-short episode. It must never fire on the absence of claims, because out-of-network, cash-pay and self-directed therapy are invisible to the ledger. Treating "no claims found" as "no therapy happened" would suppress findings for exactly the population this product is meant to protect.

  2. Built
    The guard runs on every finding, not a sample

    Verbatim quotation, exact string match against the source, failures discarded before display. Not a metric to watch but a gate to pass, which is why it's ten lines of boring code rather than anything clever.

  3. Designed, not built
    Require a citation for the link, not just the evidence

    Make the model produce a second verbatim span: the text establishing that this therapy concerns the complaint in the request. If it can't produce one, the finding is demoted rather than shown as support. The knee discharge summary physically cannot produce that span, because no sentence in it connects the episode to a lumbar problem.

    This is the piece I like most, because it reuses machinery that already exists. The same string-match verifier, pointed at relevance instead of existence.

  4. Designed, not built
    Split extraction from adjudication

    One pass finds candidate spans. A second, separate call sees the criterion, the request context and one candidate, and answers only whether it bears on that criterion, with explicit permission to reject. The knee error is a single-pass model carrying extraction momentum straight into reporting. Its own output says as much: the concern field caught the doubt, and it reported the finding anyway.

    Separating the jobs also makes the relevance judgment independently measurable, which feeds back into the harness.

  5. Designed, not built
    Treat the model's own doubt as a routing signal

    The prompt already asks the model to record uncertainty, and on the knee case it wrote down exactly the right concern before reporting anyway. That's a signal being generated and thrown away. A finding carrying a non-null concern shouldn't sit in the primary supporting-evidence slot; it belongs in a separate tier that says a judgment is needed. And how often a flagged concern turns out to matter is itself measurable, which makes it a free precision lever once calibrated.

What I'd avoid

Raising a confidence threshold, because there's no data to pick one and it trades away recall blindly. Fine-tuning on knee-versus-back examples, because that's pattern-matching to a single case. Removing the conflict display, because a reviewer seeing both sides is the safety property rather than an inconvenience. And describing any of this as a hallucination fix, since conflating a provenance failure with a relevance failure is the clearest sign someone hasn't understood their own failure taxonomy.

The one I didn't fix

The remaining miss is the strangest result in the whole exercise. A genuine find in a poor handwritten scan, where the model located the right evidence and then tidied the abbreviations into well-formed prose when quoting it. The source reads "Sympt have not resp to conserv mgmt." The quote came back as a clean sentence. The guard discarded it for quote drift, correctly.

So the guard that prevents fabrication also suppressed a real find, and the obvious repair of loosening the string match would trade away the property that makes every other citation trustworthy. Precision and recall turn out to need different medicine here, and I'd rather leave it open and say so than quietly relax the thing that makes the rest of it defensible.

The harness, the probe set and the structural checks run against synthetic cases. Labels are authored rather than physician-adjudicated, which is why inter-rater agreement is reported as unmeasured rather than estimated.

WHOOP Trial Impact Report

A personalised end-of-trial moment, designed to lift trial-to-paid conversion by giving someone a reason to see thirty days of their own data as worth paying for.

The problem

A trial user wears the device for a month, generating strain, recovery and sleep data the whole time, and never sees any of it pulled together. Then they're asked for $300 a year without an answer to the obvious question: what did I actually learn about myself? Apple Watch, Oura and Garmin are all competing for the same wrist, and the difference between converting and churning often comes down to one moment where the value is felt rather than described.

Who it's for

Two cohorts, and they need different things. The engaged trial user wore the device consistently and checked recovery daily. She's churning not because she dislikes it but because she never internalised why her own data was worth paying for. The passive user wore it and barely engaged, so the report has to surface a pattern she didn't know about rather than remind her how little she used it.

The feature

Three days before the trial ends, a full-screen report pulls thirty days into a narrative the user hasn't seen. Their recovery story, with the best and worst days and what drove each. Strain compared against similar members. Sleep patterns and one behavioural insight. The single biggest thing they couldn't have known without the device. And a forward-looking view of what another thirty days would reveal.

Tradeoffs

DecisionWhy this way
In-app, not emailEmail is easier to send and easier to ignore, and the timing is harder to control. An in-app moment arrives while the user is already inside the product deciding whether to pay. Email scoped as V2 once the in-app version proves out.
Rule-based, not MLML would find subtler patterns. An unexplainable or slightly wrong insight costs more trust than a simple correct one earns, so V1 trades sophistication for explainability and hands V2 a baseline to beat.
Minimum sample per ruleRequiring 10+ or 3+ occurrences before a rule fires means some users see a fallback instead of something personal. That's a deliberate cost. A confident-sounding insight built on two data points is noise.
Day 27, not expiry dayGives up the last three days of data in exchange for enough runway to act on the CTA before the trial lapses. A same-day report is more complete and arrives too late to change anything.
Pricing and referrals out of scopeBoth would plausibly lift conversion too, and bundling them makes it impossible to attribute any lift to this feature.

Experiment design

A/B test with a 20% holdout, sized to detect a 12.5% relative lift at 95% confidence and 80% power. A 30-day retention guardrail confirms the feature is converting genuinely engaged users rather than inflating a short-term number. Where two insight rules fire at once, the stronger effect size wins rather than the more recent one.

Rebuilding acquisition at Great Learning

Replacing broad-funnel spend at a global upskilling platform with something targeted and repeatable, and fixing the handoff that was quietly losing the warmest leads in the business.

The problem

Acquisition spend was spread across channels with no clear read on which were producing enrolments rather than clicks. And the referral programme, usually the best-converting channel a business has, was underperforming what comparable programmes manage, for reasons nobody had traced.

Who it's for

Three groups, and they behaved nothing like each other. High-intent prospects were actively comparing programmes, reading pricing and curriculum pages, close to deciding and not converting. Referred prospects were the warmest leads available and were getting stuck in a handoff that crossed three teams with no single owner. Low-intent browsers were absorbing a large share of budget relative to what they returned.

What I did

Started by auditing CRM and analytics data for which channels actually drove enrolments rather than leads, then traced where referred leads were dropping off between form submission and enrolment. The answer turned out to be delay rather than interest, so I redesigned the referral incentive into a two-sided fee concession for both the referring learner and the new enrollee, which removed the slow handoff entirely.

For segmentation I used signals already sitting in the CRM instead of building a predictive model. Less precise, and sales could use it that week. Then messaging by segment across email, sales scripts and landing pages, with one shared playbook replacing three separate team processes. I built it with sales in the room rather than handing it down, because a playbook nobody helped write is a playbook nobody follows.

What I measured

Lead-to-enrolment conversion by channel and by segment, never as an aggregate. Referral-to-enrolment isolated from paid and organic, so I could tell whether the incentive redesign moved the number it was built to move. Total pipeline volume as a guardrail, so a rising conversion rate couldn't hide a shrinking funnel while budget shifted. Cost per enrolment by channel, to confirm reallocated spend was more efficient rather than just differently allocated. And segment messaging engagement ahead of conversion data, so weak messaging surfaced early.

What the data said

Once the handoff friction was gone, referred leads converted noticeably better than paid ones, which confirmed referral had been underinvested relative to its return. Segment-specific messaging moved the needle most for high-intent users and barely registered for low-intent browsers, which supported pulling spend out of that segment rather than continuing to rewrite its copy.

The referral redesign also showed up somewhere I wasn't looking. Enrolled learners talking about the programme drove a rise in organic search and inbound queries from people who never went through a formal referral link. That's where the 15% organic lift came from, and it wasn't the effect I set out to create.
